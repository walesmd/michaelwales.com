title: Research at the Hourly Scale
date: 2026-06-24

A few months into my role as Senior Director of AI Programs at [CodePath](https://www.codepath.org/), the thing that keeps surprising me isn't the mission - it's the cadence. I expected the mission to be compelling; CodePath is reprogramming higher education to create the first generation of AI-native engineers, CTOs, and founders, centered on the needs of first-generation and low-income students. What I didn't fully appreciate until I was inside it is how seriously the organization takes the idea that you can't teach the future of tech without living it yourself.

Last month I built a simulation of how some of our programs could run under a range of conditions - varying enrollment, cohort sizes, staffing assumptions, and the messy realities that show up when you try to serve tens of thousands of students. The point wasn't to produce a pretty model; it was to surface the decisions that matter before they become expensive. The simulation gave us key insights into where programs bend, where they break, and where we have more headroom than our intuition suggested. I wrote up the work, the methodology, and what we learned in a dedicated piece: [CodePath Sim](https://michaelwales.com/codepath-sim/).

I want to highlight that work here, but honestly the more interesting story is the one underneath it. At a lot of organizations, "run a simulation to inform a strategic decision" is a quarter-long initiative that requires a special budget, a steering committee, and a slide deck to justify the time. At CodePath, this kind of research happens at the *hourly* scale. The resources, the technology, the time, and - critically - the leadership support are all there by default. You have an idea about how to make a program better, and the path from idea to evidence is measured in hours, not months.

That's not an accident. It's the same conviction that shows up in how we teach: you learn the future of computer science by doing the work, not by reading about it after the fact. We embody the future of tech not only by teaching it, but by experiencing it ourselves - building tools, running experiments, and letting the evidence change our minds. The simulation is one small example of a much broader culture where building to learn is just how the work gets done.
